Effective Stress and the Pressure Ladder

The three numbers that sit under every mud weight, casing seat, and lost-circulation event you've ever dealt with.

You've used the mud weight window your whole career. This lesson rebuilds the theory underneath it, so that overburden, pore pressure, and effective stress — and the causal chains connecting them — are at instant recall. One idea does most of the work: rock feels effective stress, not total stress.

1 · The pressure ladder

At any depth, three pressure/stress curves define the game. From lowest to highest (in a normally pressured well):

  1. Hydrostatic pore pressure — the weight of a connected column of formation water.
  2. Fracture pressure — set mostly by the minimum horizontal stress σh, it lives between the other two.
  3. Overburden (vertical stress σv) — the weight of everything above: rock grains plus pore fluid plus (offshore) the seawater column.
Pressure from a fluid column (memorize both forms) p [bar] = 0.0981 · ρ [sg] · TVD [m]    |    p [psi] = 0.1704 · ρ [lb/gal] · TVD [m]

Where do these come from? A gradient is just ρg: for freshwater, 1000 kg/m³ × 9.81 m/s² = 9.81 kPa/m = 0.0981 bar/m. Overburden is the integral of bulk density (grains + fluid, typically 1.8–2.6 g/cm³, increasing with compaction) from surface to depth — which is why the overburden gradient in EMW terms is low in shallow offshore sections (seawater + soft sediment above) and climbs toward ~2.3 sg with depth.

2 · Effective stress — the one equation

Terzaghi / Biot effective stress σ′ = σ − α·pp     (α = Biot coefficient ≈ 1 for soft/weak rocks; Terzaghi takes α = 1)

The skeleton of rock grains carries only the part of total stress not carried by the pore fluid. Everything mechanical the rock does — compacting, strengthening, failing, fracturing — responds to σ′, not σ. This single idea generates the causal chains below, which are the real payload of this lesson (PetroWiki: stress impact on rock properties, rock failure relationships).

Causal chain A — why overpressure exists and how logs see it
  1. Rapid burial of low-permeability shale → pore fluid cannot escape as fast as load is added.
  2. The fluid carries part of the new load → pp rises above hydrostatic (undercompaction / disequilibrium compaction — the dominant mechanism in young basins).
  3. σ′ = σv − pp stays anomalously low → compaction stalls → porosity stays anomalously high for that depth.
  4. High porosity → slow sonic velocity, low density, low resistivity vs. the normal compaction trend → this departure is exactly what Eaton-type pore-pressure prediction quantifies.
Causal chain B — why depleted zones take losses
  1. Production drops pp in the reservoir.
  2. σ′ rises, the rock compacts slightly, and poroelasticity pulls the minimum horizontal stress σh down with it: Δσh ≈ 0.5–0.8 · Δpp (the reservoir stress path).
  3. Fracture pressure ≈ f(σh) → the frac gradient falls almost in step with depletion.
  4. Your old mud weight now sits above the new frac gradient → losses in infill wells through depleted sands. Same physics, reversed sign, explains why the frac gradient is high in overpressured zones — and why the drilling margin narrows there.
The window, from first principles: mud weight must exceed pp (well control) and collapse pressure (σ′-driven shear failure of the wall) but stay below fracture pressure (σh-driven). Every casing seat is where these curves squeeze the window shut.
