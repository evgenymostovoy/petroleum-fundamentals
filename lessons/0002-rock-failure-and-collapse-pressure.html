Rock Failure and the Collapse Pressure

Why the mud weight window has a geomechanical lower bound — and why breakouts always know where σh points.

In Lesson 1 the window's bounds were pore pressure below and fracture pressure above. But you've raised mud weight to cure cavings plenty of times — well above pore pressure. This lesson rebuilds why: drilling a hole concentrates stress at the wall, and rock at the wall fails in shear when the mud doesn't push back hard enough. Two equations do all the work.

1 · When does rock break? Mohr–Coulomb

Mohr–Coulomb shear failure criterion τ = S0 + μ·σn′     (S0 = cohesion, μ = tan φ = internal friction)

A plane inside the rock fails when the shear stress on it beats the rock's cohesion plus friction × the effective normal stress clamping it shut. Note it is σn′ — Lesson 1's effective stress — that does the clamping: raise pore pressure and you unclamp every potential failure plane in the rock. That single idea explains everything from wellbore collapse in overpressured shale to injection-induced seismicity (PetroWiki: rock failure relationships).

μ ≈ 0.6–1.0Byerlee friction — remarkably universal across rock types
φ ≈ 30°typical friction angle (μ = tan φ ≈ 0.6)
UCS = 2S0·tan(45°+φ/2)≈ 3.5·S0 at φ = 30°
2–30 MPaUCS of weak overburden shales — the rocks that actually cause trouble

Sources for the numbers: friction from Byerlee (1978, Pure and Applied Geophysics 116 — the famous near-universality result); φ and UCS for sedimentary rocks in Fjær et al., chs. 2–3; weak-shale strength correlations from Horsrud (2001, SPE Drilling & Completion, SPE-56017).

2 · The hole concentrates stress: Kirsch

Far from the well, the rock carries σH and σh. The hole must carry the same load with rock missing — so hoop (tangential) stress concentrates at the wall. The solution is Kirsch's (1898), applied to boreholes in Fjær et al., ch. 4 and Zoback, Reservoir Geomechanics, ch. 6. For a vertical well, at the two extreme azimuths:

Kirsch — hoop stress at the wall of a vertical well max (at σh azimuth): σθ = 3σH − σh − pw
min (at σH azimuth): σθ = 3σh − σH − pw

Read the two levers. The far-field stresses set the load; the mud pressure pw is your only knob, and it appears with a minus sign: raising mud weight lowers hoop stress, substituting fluid support for the missing rock.

Causal chain A — breakouts (mud too light)
  1. Low pw → hoop stress high, and highest at the σh azimuth (the 3σH term dominates).
  2. Effective hoop stress vs. Mohr–Coulomb: the wall fails in shear on two diametrically opposed patches — breakouts, always at the σh azimuth.
  3. Cavings at the shakers; enlarged, ovalized hole on the caliper/image log.
  4. Because the azimuth is set by the stress field, image-logged breakouts are how geomechanicists measure σh orientation — your caliper data is a stress instrument (the World Stress Map is largely built this way; Zoback, ch. 6).
Causal chain B — tensile fractures (mud too heavy)
  1. High pw → the minimum hoop stress (at the σH azimuth) drops through zero into tension.
  2. When effective σθ ≤ −T0 (tensile strength, often ≈ 0 for jointed rock), the wall cracks open — drilling-induced tensile fractures, always at the σH azimuth.
  3. Push further and the fracture propagates away from the wall against σh → losses. This is Lesson 1's fracture pressure, now derived instead of memorized.
The window, completed: collapse pressure (shear at the wall) sets the practical lower bound — often above pore pressure in weak or overpressured shale. Fracture pressure (tension at the wall) sets the upper. Both are the same Kirsch equation with your mud weight sliding the hoop stress up and down. A well that cavings-in at 1.45 sg and loses at 1.65 sg is just showing you its stress state.
